Recognize · SBI

CausalPFN

Balazadeh, Kamkari, Thomas, et al.·2025
Open PDF (private repo) Back to study plan
Summary

Same PFN-for-causality idea as Do-PFN, but targets the expectation (CEPO) and enforces identifiability in the training prior. Tested on real causal benchmarks (IHDP, LaLonde, ACIC) plus real uplift tasks — more real-data validation than Do-PFN.

Study Notes

The estimand is different from Do-PFN — this is the real structural distinction

CausalPFN targets the CEPO (Conditional Expected Potential Outcome): μ_t(x) = E[Y_t|X=x] — a mean. Do-PFN targets the full conditional interventional distribution p(y|do(t),x), including its aleatoric noise. This isn't a minor framing difference: CausalPFN's choice isolates epistemic uncertainty about the causal effect itself, while Do-PFN's choice captures the full outcome distribution's spread too. Everything else about the two papers' designs follows from this one choice.

A different prior-construction philosophy: retrofit real tables, don't simulate from scratch

Where Do-PFN samples fresh random structural causal models from scratch, CausalPFN starts from 337 existing OpenML tabular datasets (checked for leakage) plus synthetic TabPFN-style tables, and transforms each into a valid causal dataset: pick covariates X, relabel two other columns as μ₀(X)/μ₁(X) (optionally adding noise), sample treatment T from a random propensity function of X via a sigmoid (guaranteeing 0 < P(T=1|X) < 1, i.e. positivity), and form the observed outcome Y := Y_T. Because treatment is constructed to depend only on X, strong ignorability holds by design, not by hope — every training example is automatically identifiable.

A proven consistency result, via the same proof technique as Do-PFN

Theorem 1 (formalized in the appendix): CEPO-PPD estimation is consistent — recovers the true CEPO as data grows — if and only if the prior is "CEPO-identifiable," meaning it only samples DGPs whose CEPOs are functions of the observational distribution alone. Proven via Doob's martingale convergence theorem — independently, the same proof technique Do-PFN uses for its own consistency result, a genuine methodological convergence between two concurrent, competing papers. Two design principles the theorem implies directly: the prior must rule out non-identifiable cases (which CausalPFN's construction does structurally), and broadening the prior's coverage increases the chance the real DGP falls within its support.

A genuinely elegant solution to an otherwise intractable calibration problem

CATE ground truth is never observable at test time — you can't check calibration against something you can never measure. CausalPFN's workaround: define "regression calibration" using the observed factual outcome y against the model's predicted interval for μ_t(x) (which is checkable, since y is directly observed) as a computable proxy. The paper proves — and empirically confirms across all tested DGPs — that regression-calibration error is always ≤ the (unmeasurable) CATE-calibration error. So making the checkable proxy well-calibrated (via temperature scaling on the softmax output, fit with 5-fold calibration on observational data alone, no counterfactuals needed) guarantees the unmeasurable quantity is at least as good. Confirmed empirically: raw CausalPFN is well-calibrated in-distribution but "becomes severely overconfident" out-of-distribution — the same overconfidence-under-shift pattern this whole library keeps documenting — and temperature scaling via the proxy makes that overconfidence disappear.

Results: best average CATE rank across 310 realizations, real uplift-marketing validation

Across IHDP, ACIC 2016, and two Lalonde cohorts (310 total realizations), CausalPFN achieves the best average CATE rank of every baseline tested (T-/S-/X-/DA-Learner, DragonNet, TarNet, RA-Net, IPW, BART, GRF, Forest DML/DR-Learner) — while trained entirely on simulated data and never seeing the evaluation data during pretraining. The paper's own framing: individual specialized baselines "perform well on specific datasets but underperform on others" — CausalPFN's consistency across all of them is the actual point, not beating any one baseline by the widest margin. On five real marketing RCTs (Hillstrom, Lenta, Retail Hero/X5, Megafon, Criteo — evaluated via Qini curves, the real industry-standard uplift-modeling metric), CausalPFN achieves the best mean normalized Qini score (0.952) against X-Learner (0.944), DA-Learner (0.912), S-Learner (0.881), and T-Learner (0.876) — though honestly, performance drops on the very largest un-subsampled tables (millions of rows), attributed explicitly to the known context-length scalability limits of PFN-style transformers.

A controlled ablation isolating the value of the causal-specific prior

Comparing against plain TabPFN (the Nature-paper v2, using its regression output as a naive CEPO proxy): "TabPFN is surprisingly competitive without any causal tuning, yet CausalPFN outperforms it on every benchmark except ACIC 2016." To isolate whether the gain comes from the causal-specific prior rather than just more training, the authors fine-tune plain TabPFN on CausalPFN's own causal prior for 48 hours — this "causal fine-tuning boosts the performance," confirming the prior design itself, not just extra compute, is what's doing the work.

The direct rivalry with Do-PFN — stated plainly, both sides

CausalPFN's own related-work section names Do-PFN directly: "concurrent work by Robertson et al. also applies PFNs to causal effect estimation but lacks a procedure to guarantee the identifiability of the prior data; additionally, we observe relatively poor empirical performance compared to CausalPFN." That's a real, pointed critique worth reporting precisely rather than picking a side. The fairer full picture, cross-referencing Do-PFN's own stated scope: Do-PFN's prior deliberately does not restrict itself to identifiable cases — it's explicitly built and tested to handle unobserved-confounder scenarios too, correctly inflating its uncertainty when the causal effect is genuinely unidentifiable. CausalPFN's prior structurally cannot represent that scenario at all — every training example is constructed to satisfy strong ignorability by design. The two papers are answering different-sized questions: CausalPFN targets a narrower, well-identified problem class with a tighter, better-validated, better-calibrated solution within that scope; Do-PFN targets a broader class including genuine unidentifiability, at the cost of a less mature real-data validation record. "Better" depends on which problem you actually have.

Resolving an apparent tension with tabpfn25.html

TabPFN-2.5's own report found a generic TabPFN-2.5-as-T-Learner beating CausalPFN on RealCause. That's not a contradiction of this paper's own claim to beat TabPFN — it's a timeline: this paper (Oct 2025) benchmarks against the older TabPFN v2 (the Nature paper) and beats it on nearly every benchmark; TabPFN-2.5 (Nov 2025, released after this paper) is a materially stronger base model, and a generic T-Learner wrapper around that newer model is what later surpassed CausalPFN. Worth tracking as a live, ongoing horse race rather than a settled question — the frontier tabular base model keeps moving, and each purpose-built causal architecture's advantage is measured against whatever the current generic baseline happens to be at publication time.

Explicit, stated limitations

Fundamentally assumes strong ignorability, which is untestable in practice — "without this condition, CausalPFN has no guarantees of validity... domain expertise still remains essential" to judge whether the method is even appropriate for a given problem. Theoretical guarantees rely on idealized assumptions (well-specified prior, asymptotically large datasets) — no finite-sample theory yet. Currently implemented only for binary treatments (the framework supports multi-arm discrete treatments in principle; continuous treatment is fully unexplored). Extending to richer domain-informed priors (e.g. instrumental variables) is named as future work, and non-trivial to design scalably.

Discussion, Section by Section

Plain-English walkthrough (start here)

Read dopfn.html first. This paper is best understood as a sibling with a deliberately narrower, more disciplined scope — same underlying idea (train a transformer on millions of fake causal worlds), different bet about what to promise.

1. Averages vs. full shapes

Imagine two ways to answer "what happens if we give this customer a discount": one gives you the full range of possible outcomes with all their randomness (some customers buy a lot more, some barely react, here's the whole spread) — that's Do-PFN's approach. The other gives you just the single best-guess average effect — that's this paper's approach. The narrower promise turns out to be easier to build a really solid, well-tested, well-calibrated tool around, which is exactly what this paper spends its effort doing.

2. Guaranteeing the easy cases are actually easy, instead of hoping

Rather than generating random cause-and-effect diagrams and hoping most of them turn out to be the "nice," solvable kind, this paper builds its training examples by starting from real spreadsheets and deliberately constructing the treatment-assignment rule so that the resulting problem is always solvable by design — like a teacher who writes exam questions specifically ensuring every one has a clean answer, rather than including a few unsolvable trick questions and hoping students realize which ones to skip. That's a real trade-off: you get a tool that's reliable and well-tested within its guaranteed scope, but it structurally can't handle the harder, murkier scenarios that fall outside that scope.

3. A clever way to check your work when the answer key doesn't exist

Normally you'd calibrate a model by comparing its predictions to the true answer on held-out data. But the true "what would have happened under the other treatment" is never actually observable for any real person — you only ever see what happened under the treatment they actually got. This paper's neat trick: use the one thing you can check (did the observed outcome fall inside the model's predicted range) as a stand-in, and prove mathematically that fixing the checkable version also fixes the unfixable one. It's a bit like being unable to weigh a package directly but proving that if you get its shipping-label weight estimate right, the true weight is guaranteed to be at least as accurate — a genuinely clever workaround for a problem that looks unsolvable at first glance.

4. Two competing papers, disagreeing in public, both worth taking seriously

It's worth noticing plainly: this paper directly says it outperforms a concurrent rival (Do-PFN) and names a specific reason why. That's normal, healthy scientific disagreement, not a sign either paper is untrustworthy — but it's worth reading critically rather than taking the winning paper's self-assessment as the final word. Once you look at what each paper actually promises to solve, the "which one is better" question turns out to have a real answer: it depends on whether your actual problem has a hidden, unmeasured backstory or not.

Going deeper (revisit once the basics above feel solid)

Worth holding three threads together at once, now that Module 04 is underway: (1) the Do-PFN vs. CausalPFN rivalry, and which one's assumptions actually fit a given real problem; (2) the TabPFN-2.5-beats-CausalPFN finding from tabpfn25.html, now precisely dated as a later, stronger base model overtaking an earlier purpose-built architecture, not a refutation of the causal-specific-prior idea itself; and (3) the open question both papers leave unresolved — as generic tabular foundation models keep getting better, does a purpose-built causal architecture's advantage shrink toward zero, or does it persist specifically in the genuinely hard cases (unidentifiability, calibrated uncertainty under confounding) that a generic wrapper has no mechanism to represent at all?
