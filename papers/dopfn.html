Do-PFN

Robertson, Reuter, Guo, Hollmann, Hutter, Schölkopf·2025·NeurIPS
Open PDF (private repo) Back to study plan
Summary

Trains a transformer on millions of synthetic Structural Causal Models so it can predict conditional interventional distributions p(y | do(t), x) zero-shot, without the causal graph at inference time. Correctly performs back-door/front-door-style adjustment implicitly. Synthetic/semi-synthetic evidence only so far.

Study Notes

What's genuinely new versus TabPFN: two SCMs, not one

TabPFN trains on synthetic datasets from a single, consistent data-generating process. Do-PFN's training loop (Algorithm 1) simulates two datasets per training example from the same sampled SCM ψ: an observational dataset D_ob (the SCM run normally) and an interventional dataset D_in (the same SCM with incoming edges to the treatment variable removed and its value fixed — a genuine do(t) intervention). The transformer is trained to predict interventional outcomes y_in given (t_in, x_in) and the entire observational dataset as context — learning to bridge from "what was observed" to "what would happen under intervention" purely through exposure to millions of these paired simulations. Architecturally, this is TabPFN plus one addition: a special indicator marking which input column is the treatment variable. Do-PFN has just 7.3M parameters — notably tiny next to TabPFN-2.5/3's scale, trained in 48–96 hours on a single consumer GPU (an RTX 2080).

Two proven theoretical results, not just empirical claims

  • Optimality (Prop. 1): SGD training provably minimizes the expected forward-KL divergence between the true conditional interventional distribution (CID) and the model's approximation — in expectation over the synthetic prior. The paper is explicit about the caveat this carries (the same one tcdbi.html's Corollary made for the general PFN case): the guarantee is about the synthetic data-generating process, and real-world transfer relies on empirical evidence from the wider TabPFN lineage, not a separate proof.
  • Consistency (Prop. 3): as the observational dataset grows (|D_ob| → ∞), Do-PFN's predicted CID converges — proven rigorously via Doob's martingale convergence theorem — to the true CID up to the observational-equivalence class of the true SCM. In plain terms: it doesn't magically resolve genuine unidentifiability; if multiple causal structures are consistent with infinite observational data, Do-PFN converges to correctly averaging over that irreducible ambiguity, not to a single wrong-but-confident answer.

Three distinct sources of uncertainty, cleanly separated (Eq. 4)

(1) Aleatoric — genuine randomness in the SCM's own outcome mechanism; (2) unidentifiability uncertainty — arising because multiple observationally-equivalent SCMs could have generated the same data, a genuinely causal-specific uncertainty type with no analog in TabPFN's purely predictive setting; (3) epistemic uncertainty about which equivalence class the true SCM belongs to, given only finite observational data. This decomposition is a real conceptual clarification, not just bookkeeping — it's what lets the paper later show uncertainty correctly increasing under genuine unidentifiability (below).

A clean ablation proving the pretraining captures something genuinely causal

"Dont-PFN" — the paper's own ablation — is a regression model pretrained on the identical synthetic prior but never shown interventional data during training. Do-PFN substantially outperforms Dont-PFN. That's a real, controlled test: it isolates "seeing interventions during pretraining" as the source of the improvement, rather than just "more/better synthetic data of any kind."

Correct causal adjustment without ever seeing the graph

Across six deliberately constructed case studies (observed confounder, observed mediator, confounder+mediator, unobserved confounder, front-door criterion, back-door criterion — Fig. 2), Do-PFN is given only observational data, no graph. It performs competitively with "DoWhy" baselines that are given the true graph and apply classical front-door/back-door adjustment explicitly. Further evidence this isn't a fluke: "Do-PFN-Graph" — a version additionally pretrained on the ground-truth graph structure for each case study — doesn't meaningfully outperform the graph-agnostic version. The model appears to have learned to perform the appropriate adjustment implicitly, from data patterns alone.

CATE results, robustness, and a genuinely subtle honest finding

Do-PFN significantly outperforms meta-learners (S/T/X-learner), causal-forest double machine learning, and deep-learning methods (DragonNet, TARNet) on conditional average treatment effect estimation — and stays notably more consistent when the unconfoundedness assumption is violated, where the baseline methods (built around that assumption) degrade sharply. Under unobserved confounding specifically, Do-PFN performs closer to the DoWhy gold standard on CATE than on raw CID prediction — and the paper doesn't just report this, it explains it: a bias-decomposition analysis shows Do-PFN slightly over-predicts the CID in a way that cancels out when computing the CATE (a difference of two similarly-biased predictions). A genuinely subtle result, reported rather than left as an unexplained anomaly.

Uncertainty calibration checked directly, with the expected failure mode present and explained

Prediction-interval coverage analysis confirms Do-PFN is overall well-calibrated. In the unobserved-confounder case study specifically, its uncertainty correctly increases — exactly matching the theoretical expectation that unidentifiability should inflate uncertainty, and the paper states it "learns to exactly capture this increase." On identifiable case studies, Do-PFN is slightly under-confident — attributed honestly to two named, specific causes: discretization error from the bar-distribution (Riemann-style) posterior representation, and forward-KL training not being perfectly minimized in practice, both cited to prior theory rather than left unexplained.

Out-of-distribution robustness, and a fix that mirrors TabPFN-3's own pattern

Robust to different noise distributions (Laplace, Student-t, Gumbel, mixed) and different non-linearities — though doubled Gaussian noise variance specifically hurts performance. Do-PFN v1's performance degrades on graphs larger than it was trained on (>20 nodes); this is directly recovered by v1.1's larger-scale pretraining (graphs up to 60 nodes) — the same "identify a specific weakness, fix it by widening the prior's range" pattern TabPFN-3 used for its own high-frequency-oscillation weakness.

Real and semi-real validation

On the RealCause benchmark (semi-synthetic, built from real covariates in ACIC2016/LaLonde/IHDP data, and specifically constructed to perfectly satisfy unconfoundedness — inherently advantaging the baseline methods built around that exact assumption), Do-PFN remains competitive with meta-learner, doubly-robust, and deep-learning CATE estimators despite making no special-case assumption, and achieves a Pareto-optimal speed/accuracy trade-off. On two real datasets with a known causal graph — the Law School Admissions racial-bias study and an Amazon Sales mediation study — Do-PFN achieves the strongest performance on Law School and is not significantly worse than the best method on Amazon Sales.

Explicit, honest limitations

Generalization critically depends on how well the synthetic SCM prior matches real-world causal complexity — validation so far is synthetic/semi-synthetic, and "principled methods for prior design and validation" are named as needed future work. The statistical theory around guarantees for amortized causal models is explicitly called "still developing," less mature than for classical non-amortized estimators under known structure. Scope is narrow: binary interventions only, no broader intervention types, no counterfactuals, i.i.d. data only.

Discussion, Section by Section

Plain-English walkthrough (start here)

Read tabpfn.html first for the core recipe. This paper asks a harder version of TabPFN's question: not "what value is probably in this blank cell," but "what would this cell's value probably become if we reached in and changed something upstream of it."

1. Two versions of the same fake world, shown together

Recall the fake-spreadsheet training trick from TabPFN. This paper generates fake worlds twice per training round: once left alone (observational — just watch what naturally happens), and once with one variable forcibly set to a chosen value, as if a scientist reached in and changed it directly (interventional — do this and see what happens). The network gets to see the natural version and is quizzed on what the forced version would produce. Do that with millions of different fake worlds, each with a different hidden cause-and-effect wiring diagram, and the network starts to pick up something more general than "predict the next number" — it starts to pick up "predict what changes when you force something."

2. Learning to do textbook causal reasoning without being taught the textbook rules

There's a whole formal branch of statistics (do-calculus) with named recipes for correctly estimating cause-and-effect from data alone, depending on the specific shape of the hidden causal wiring — one recipe if there's a confounding third variable pulling the strings behind the scenes, a different one if the effect flows through a middleman variable. Normally you'd need to know the wiring diagram to pick the right recipe. This paper's real finding is that the network learns to behave as if it knows the diagram and picks the correct recipe automatically, purely by having seen the pattern of "observational data in, correct interventional answer out" enough times — without ever being shown a single actual diagram.

3. A kind of uncertainty this whole library hasn't needed to talk about before

Every earlier calibration discussion in this library was about "is the model too sure of itself, given what it could possibly know." This paper introduces a genuinely different flavor: sometimes the data itself simply cannot distinguish between two different underlying true stories, no matter how much of it you collect — an honest "I truly cannot tell from this evidence alone" rather than "I haven't seen enough evidence yet." The paper shows its model's uncertainty correctly balloons specifically in exactly the scenario built to be genuinely unresolvable (the hidden, unmeasured common cause) — a good sign the network has learned to recognize the difference between "uncertain because data is limited" and "uncertain because the question is fundamentally unanswerable from this angle."

4. When two mistakes cancel out, and why that's worth explaining rather than hiding

Sometimes a model is wrong about two related quantities in the same direction and by a similar amount — and if you only ever care about the difference between those two quantities, the errors partly cancel, leaving the difference more accurate than either quantity was alone. That's exactly what happens here: the model's raw predictions under intervention are a bit off in the hardest scenario, but because a treatment-effect estimate is a difference of two such predictions, much of that error washes out. Reporting this explicitly — rather than just quietly showing the good treatment-effect numbers — is the kind of honesty worth noticing and expecting from a paper.

Going deeper (revisit once the basics above feel solid)

Worth carrying forward a real tension once CausalPFN (next in this reading order) and tabpfn25.html are both fresh: TabPFN-2.5's own report found a generic TabPFN-2.5 model, wrapped in an old-fashioned statistical recipe (a T-Learner), beating CausalPFN — a purpose-built causal architecture — on the RealCause benchmark's point-estimate accuracy. This paper's own uncertainty-decomposition results (correctly inflating uncertainty under genuine unidentifiability) are exactly the kind of qualitative capability a generic wrapper around an ordinary model likely can't replicate — worth treating that as the live, open question when comparing purpose-built causal architectures against "just use a really good general model": is the advantage about point-estimate accuracy (where the generic wrapper may already win) or about knowing when you don't know (where a purpose-built architecture like this one might still have the edge)?
