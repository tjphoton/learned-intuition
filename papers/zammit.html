Neural Methods for Amortized Inference

Zammit-Mangion, Sainsbury-Dale, Huser·2025·Annual Review of Statistics
Open PDF (private repo) Back to study plan
Summary

Unifies neural point-estimation, posterior-approximation, and likelihood/ratio-approximation methods under one decision-theoretic "amortization" framework, with a worked example benchmarking seven amortized methods against MCMC. The 90% variational-posterior interval covering only 77.7% of the time — despite using the exact likelihood — is the sharpest illustration of the calibration risk in this whole library.

Study Notes

What this paper actually reviews (confirmed from the text)

The paper positions itself against Cranmer et al. (2020) in two specific, stated ways: it only covers methods that use neural networks, and only ones that are amortized — a fast feed-forward relationship between data and the inferential target, built at an upfront training cost. It opens by literally quoting the Oxford English Dictionary: amortization is "the action or practice of gradually writing off the initial cost of an asset." Four content sections follow: point estimation and approximate posteriors (Sec. 3), neural summary statistics (Sec. 4), neural likelihood and likelihood-to-evidence ratio (Sec. 5), and software plus one real worked example (Sec. 6).

Amortization as decision theory (Sec. 2)

A decision rule δ(·) maps any input X to an answer; usually you'd solve for the best δ separately for every X, which is expensive to repeat. Brown & Purves (1973) show that, under mild conditions, a single closed-form rule δ*(·) exists that is simultaneously optimal for every X — build it once, reuse it forever. The property that makes this learnable: δ*(·) also minimizes the average loss over X drawn from any distribution ("average optimality"). That's the exact mathematical fact that licenses "train a neural network on a big batch of simulated examples" as a way to approximate δ*, instead of solving an separate optimization for every new input.

Neural Bayes Estimators (Sec. 3.1)

A point estimator θ̂(·), trained via ordinary empirical risk minimization on N simulated (θ, Z) pairs, to minimize posterior expected loss (Bayes risk) under a chosen loss function L. What the network converges to depends entirely on L: squared-error loss → posterior mean; a specific variance-of-error loss (Fan & Yao, 1998) → posterior variance; a quantile ("pinball") loss → a posterior quantile — chain a few quantile losses together and you get calibrated credible intervals without ever building a full posterior density. Concrete number from the paper: Sainsbury-Dale et al. obtain posterior medians and 95% credible intervals for Gaussian-process covariance parameters across 2,000+ spatial regions of sea-surface temperature in about three minutes on a single GPU.

Named failure mode, used throughout the rest of the review: the amortization gap — the discrepancy between the trained network and the true, theoretical Bayes estimator, caused by incomplete training (too few simulations), insufficient network flexibility, or both.

Approximate posteriors via KL-divergence minimization (Sec. 3.2)

Forward KL minimization tends to produce over-dispersed approximations that cover every mode of the true posterior. Reverse KL minimization ("variational Bayes") tends to produce under-dispersed, mode-seeking approximations that lock onto a single mode and underestimate spread. In simulation-based settings the paper favors forward KL: it's natively likelihood-free and simpler to implement with normalizing flows. Reverse KL needs the actual likelihood p(Z|θ) inside its training objective, so when that's intractable it gets paired with a neural-likelihood surrogate (Sec. 5.1).

Neural summary statistics (Sec. 4)

Explicit construction: train a "summary network" to maximize mutual information between θ and the learned statistic S(Z) — high mutual information means S(Z) is far from independent of θ, i.e. highly informative. Directly computing this is intractable, so the paper covers the MINE estimator (a contrastive objective trained against randomly shuffled "independent" pairs) and a more stable Jensen–Shannon variant.

Implicit construction: split the network into a summary network (data → compressed statistic) and an inference network (statistic → posterior/likelihood parameters), trained end to end together — the same two-stage shape as metabeta's local/global summary networks, named here as a general design pattern (Fig. 4).

How many statistics is a genuine open design choice: some fix the count equal to the number of model parameters, some double it; Sainsbury-Dale et al. use 128 — far more than needed — and let training down-weight the irrelevant ones.

Neural likelihood and likelihood-to-evidence ratio (Sec. 5)

Three stated reasons to target the likelihood, or the ratio r(θ,Z) = p(Z|θ)/p(Z), instead of the posterior directly: (1) likelihoods and likelihood ratios are the currency of frequentist inference, hypothesis testing, and MCMC transition probabilities; (2) for conditionally i.i.d. replicate data, the multi-replicate ratio is just the product of single-replicate ratios — a network trained on one replicate generalizes to any number of them at inference time, with zero architecture changes, unlike posterior-based approaches; (3) these approximators are prior-free by construction, so one trained network is reusable across multiple different priors on the same model.

The ratio-estimation trick (Sec. 5.2) is genuinely elegant: train an ordinary binary classifier to tell apart real, matched (θ, Z) pairs from artificially mismatched ones — generated for free by randomly shuffling Z's against θ's within a training batch. The classifier's output probability converts algebraically into the likelihood ratio: r(θ,Z) = c*(θ,Z) / (1 − c*(θ,Z)).

Safety variant with a direct forward link: Delaunoy et al. (2022) — sharing an author, Hermans, with "A Trust Crisis in Simulation-Based Inference" (Module 03) — propose balanced neural ratio estimation, penalizing the classifier during training unless it satisfies a symmetry condition that mathematically forces it toward conservative, less-overconfident outputs.

Joint amortization — targeting posterior and likelihood together in one training run: JANA (Radev et al. 2023a), and Simformer (Gloeckler et al. 2024), which is already Module 02 in this library, using a score-based diffusion model over the joint distribution of θ and Z.

Software landscape (Sec. 6.1)

  • NeuralEstimators (Julia/Flux, R interface) — neural Bayes estimators + ratio approximators, built for exchangeable/replicate data, with bootstrap uncertainty and censored/missing-data handling.
  • sbi (Python/PyTorch) — posterior, likelihood, or ratio targets; amortized and sequential training; used later in Module 02.
  • LAMPE (PyTorch) — posterior or ratio, with on-disk data streaming for very large training sets.
  • BayesFlow (TensorFlow) — posterior, likelihood, or joint (JANA); misspecification detection; amortized Bayesian model comparison — covered directly in Module 02.
  • swyft (PyTorch) — ratio estimation for subsets of parameters specifically.

The worked example (Sec. 6.2) — a real head-to-head, with real numbers

One unknown parameter θ (a spatial correlation length scale, prior Uniform(0, 0.6)), a 16×16 grid of simulated spatial data, seven methods against a non-amortized MCMC gold standard: a neural Bayes estimator (NBE), a forward-KL amortized posterior (fKL), three reverse-KL variational variants differing only in which likelihood stand-in they use (rKL1 = the true likelihood, rKL2 = a synthetic likelihood from a hand-picked "expert" summary statistic, rKL3 = a synthetic likelihood from a mutual-information-learned statistic), and a neural ratio estimator (NRE).

Speed: every neural method trains in a few minutes on a laptop CPU, then runs inference in a few dozen milliseconds per dataset — MCMC takes roughly a minute per dataset. Call it a thousand-to-several-thousand-fold speedup, with accuracy close to, but consistently slightly behind, MCMC (the "amortization gap" in practice).

Calibration — the number worth remembering: a well-calibrated 90% credible interval should cover the truth about 90% of the time. MCMC: 89.6%. NRE: 91.3%. But rKL1 — reverse-KL variational inference using the exact true likelihood, no approximation at all — only achieves 77.7% coverage. The miscalibration isn't from an approximate likelihood; it comes from reverse KL's mode-seeking, under-dispersed behavior described in Sec. 3.2. A fully concrete, numeric instance of the calibration warning that recurs across this whole library.

Epilogue — open problems, with a direct line to metabeta

Two flagged open problems stand out: (1) recovering distributions of random effects in hierarchical models by combining amortized latent-variable inference with the parameter-estimation methods this review covers — precisely the problem metabeta (Module 02) takes up, one paper later in this reading order; (2) whether ABC itself can be amortized, and whether that has any advantage over the neural methods surveyed here — explicitly left open.

Discussion, Section by Section

Plain-English walkthrough (start here)

This builds directly on the vocabulary from Cranmer et al. — prior, likelihood, posterior, simulator, and the idea of training a network on simulated examples instead of hand-deriving a formula. If any of those feel shaky, that page's walkthrough is the place to revisit first.

1. What "amortized" literally means, in the paper's own example

The paper opens by quoting a dictionary: amortization is "the action or practice of gradually writing off the initial cost of an asset." It illustrates this with a real example, not a hypothetical: training the large language model BLOOM took over a million hours of GPU computing, several months, and roughly the yearly electricity use of 72 households. But once trained, using it — asking it one question — costs less energy than boiling a cup of tea. Enormous cost, paid once; tiny cost, paid every time after. Every method in this paper is that same trade, applied to statistical inference instead of language models: pay for expensive training once, then get near-instant answers forever after.

2. A "decision rule" is just a function that answers a question — the paper formalizes when you can build one, once, that works for every input

Imagine you have a cost function that tells you how bad a guess is, and it depends on both your guess and the situation you're guessing about. Normally, for each new situation, you'd have to re-solve "what's the best guess here" from scratch. The paper leans on a 1973 mathematical result (Brown & Purves) that says: under mild conditions, there exists one single rule that gives the best guess for every possible situation at once — you just have to find that rule, once, and then you can plug in any new situation and get the right answer immediately, no re-solving required.

Here's the part that makes this trainable by a neural network rather than just a nice theorem: that same best-possible rule also happens to be the one that does best on average, across a huge pile of situations sampled at random. So instead of solving an impossible-sounding "find the rule that's optimal for literally every situation" problem, you can instead just... generate a huge pile of random situations, and train a network to do as well as possible on average across all of them. That's ordinary neural network training. The 1973 theorem is what guarantees that doing this actually gets you close to the true optimal rule, not just something plausible-looking.

3. Neural Bayes Estimators: choosing your penalty decides your answer

Sometimes you don't need Cranmer's full NPE machinery (a whole posterior distribution) — you just want one good number: "what's my best single guess for θ," or "give me a range I'm 95% confident contains the truth." A network trained to output just that is called a neural Bayes estimator.

How does the network know what "best" means? Through the penalty you choose for being wrong (the loss function) — and this is genuinely just common sense once you see it. Imagine a game show where you guess a hidden number and get penalized by how wrong you are. If the penalty is "your error, squared" (big misses hurt disproportionately), your smartest strategy — averaged over everything you think is possible — is to guess the average of all the possibilities you believe in, weighted by how likely each is. That average is exactly the posterior mean, and that's genuinely what the paper proves the optimal network converges to under squared-error loss.

Change the penalty to something asymmetric ("losing more for guessing too high than too low"), and the smartest strategy shifts to guessing a specific percentile instead of the average — which is exactly how the paper builds calibrated credible intervals, by training separate networks against this kind of "quantile" penalty. Real number from the paper: this is fast enough that researchers estimated medians and 95% intervals across 2,000-plus separate spatial regions of real sea-surface-temperature data in about three minutes, on a single GPU.

4. Summary statistics: letting the network decide what's worth keeping

Recall ABC's weak spot from Cranmer's paper — it needs hand-picked summary statistics, and bad ones quietly throw away information. This paper's answer: train a network to invent good summary statistics itself, by rewarding it for maximizing "mutual information" between the statistic and θ. Intuitively — if knowing the statistic tells you nothing extra about θ beyond a blind guess, mutual information is near zero (a useless statistic); if the statistic essentially pins down θ, mutual information is high (a great statistic). The network is simply rewarded for pushing this number up, using simulated data where the true θ is already known.

5. Why sometimes target the likelihood, or a "ratio," instead of the posterior directly

Here's a genuinely practical reason, straight from the paper: imagine your real data is many independent repeats of the same kind of measurement — say, 50 sensor readings instead of 1. If you've trained a network to estimate the ratio p(data | θ) / p(data) for a single measurement, you get the ratio for all 50 measurements together for free, just by multiplying the 50 single-measurement ratios — no retraining, no architecture change. A posterior-based network doesn't get this for free; it generally has to be built and trained around a fixed number of measurements from the start.

The way the paper trains a network to estimate this ratio is a neat trick: rather than directly estimating a ratio of two hard-to-compute densities, train an ordinary yes/no classifier — "does this (θ, data) pair genuinely belong together, or were they randomly thrown together?" Generating the "randomly thrown together" examples costs nothing extra: just shuffle the data against the parameter values within a training batch. A classifier that gets very good at this yes/no game turns out, through a short piece of algebra, to have implicitly learned the ratio all along.

6. The worked example — a real stopwatch-and-scorecard comparison

The paper doesn't just claim these methods work — it builds one small, concrete problem (a single hidden number controlling how smooth a random spatial pattern looks) and runs seven different methods against a slow-but-exact reference (MCMC) on identical data. The fast methods each take a few minutes to train, then answer new questions in a few dozen milliseconds — MCMC takes about a minute per question. That's the "amortization" payoff, made concrete: pay once, then get answers roughly a thousand times faster from then on.

But here's the finding worth sitting with: a well-calibrated 90%-confidence range should be right about 90% of the time. The slow, exact method (MCMC) gets 89.6% — right on target. One of the fast methods, using the mathematically exact likelihood (not an approximation at all!), only gets 77.7% — meaningfully overconfident. The problem wasn't an approximate formula; it was the shape of the approximation strategy itself (this method assumes the answer looks roughly bell-curve-shaped and homes in on its single peak, which tends to underestimate how spread-out the true uncertainty really is). The lesson: using exact math somewhere in your pipeline doesn't automatically protect the final answer's confidence from being wrong. This is the exact same worry Cranmer's paper flagged as an open problem in 2020 — here it shows up as one specific number in one specific table.
