Summary

Scales to 1M training rows, adds a "Thinking" test-time-compute mode, extends to time-series, relational, and tabular-text data.

Study Notes

The biggest change: a genuine architectural pivot, not just scaling up

TabPFN v2 through TabPFN-2.5 (and an intermediate "TabPFN-2.6" this report cites as a baseline but which isn't otherwise covered in this reading order) all used alternating row-wise and feature-wise attention layers over every cell. TabPFN-3 abandons that design because it "becomes prohibitively expensive as the dataset size grows," and returns to something structurally closer to the original PFN idea — in-context learning over whole-row embeddings — but gets there via a new two-stage compression pipeline adapted from the TabICLv2 architecture: (1) a column-wise feature distribution embedder processes each feature column independently via inducing-point attention (avoiding full quadratic cross-row cost); (2) a row-wise feature aggregator uses learned CLS tokens to distill each row's feature embeddings into one fixed-size vector, decoupling the expensive in-context-learning stage from the number of features entirely. Only after this compression does the model do ICL — over a sequence whose length depends only on the number of rows, not rows × features. This is why TabPFN-3 scales to 1,000,000 rows where TabPFN-2.5 topped out at 100,000: it's a different shape of computation, not the same shape run on a bigger GPU.

Answering TabPFN's original class-count limit

Every earlier TabPFN version used a fixed-width classification head, capping classification natively at a small number of classes. TabPFN-3 replaces this with an attention-based retrieval decoder: it treats predicting a test point's class as soft nearest-neighbor retrieval over the training set's one-hot labels, weighted by attention (inspired by Arbel et al. and Koshil et al.). This makes the decoder non-parametric in the number of classes — though a hard ceiling of 160 classes remains from how the label embeddings were sized at pretraining time, so "arbitrary number of classes" is a training-time knob to raise, not a truly unbounded capability yet.

Production-serving optimizations: row-chunking and a small KV-cache

Row-chunking decouples peak GPU memory from dataset size by streaming rows through the compression stages in fixed-size slices while reusing precomputed inducing states — this alone gives roughly a 5× peak-memory reduction at the largest shapes. Combined with a much smaller key-value cache (achieved via multi-query attention — test rows share a single KV head instead of one per attention head, an 8× cache-size reduction), a cached prediction becomes one to three orders of magnitude faster than a cold fit-and-predict pass, at roughly 7GB of cache per estimator even for 1-million-row datasets — small enough to fit on common GPUs. This is the direct answer to the "TabPFN combines training and inference in one forward pass, which can make production serving too slow" problem that earlier versions never fully solved.

Eight new capabilities added to the synthetic SCM prior

Beyond broader graph structures and new ways of combining parent-node values, the prior gains: (1) richer categorical variable modeling (replacing a comparatively simple treatment in earlier versions); (2) fixed high-frequency oscillation handling — the report explicitly admits TabPFN-2.5 "struggled with high-frequency oscillations despite performing well on sinusoidal data generally"; (3) a spatial prior for datasets with latitude/longitude or sensor-grid structure; (4) a many-class prior matching the new decoder's capabilities; (5) a temporal prior — extending the SCM into a discrete-time Dynamic Structural Causal Model for time-ordered data, feeding directly into the TabPFN-TS line; (6) an explicit out-of-distribution/extrapolation prior, training the model to remain performant under distribution shift and to extrapolate rather than only interpolate — flagged as "notably absent from most tree-based algorithms as well as most other tabular foundation models." The final model was trained on more than 8 trillion tokens of this synthetic data.

TabPFN-3-Plus and "Thinking mode" — test-time compute scaling, imported from the LLM world

A proprietary, API/enterprise-only variant. TabPFN-3-Plus adds native text-feature support — raw string columns (product names, claim descriptions, reviews) encoded jointly with numeric/categorical features inside the model, no upstream featurization required. "Thinking mode" applies additional inference-time computation to push prediction quality further, explicitly described as achieving its gains "without using LLMs, real data, internet search, or any other model besides TabPFN" — i.e., the same test-time-compute-scaling idea behind reasoning-style LLMs (think longer at inference time for a better answer), applied to a tabular foundation model instead of a language model.

Headline benchmark numbers

  • TabArena (all 51 datasets): TabPFN-3 outperforms every other model in one forward pass, gaining 72 Elo over tuned-and-ensembled Real-TabPFN-2.5. TabPFN-3-Plus (Thinking) beats any non-TabPFN model by over 200 Elo and beats AutoGluon 1.5 extreme — a complex ensemble tuned for 4 hours that includes TabPFN v2 — by over 100 Elo, while running 10× faster.
  • TabArena's 15 largest datasets (10k–100k rows): TabPFN-3 beats every other model by over 100 Elo; TabPFN-3-Plus (Thinking) beats every non-TabPFN model by over 420 Elo and AutoGluon 1.5 extreme by 220 Elo, with a 99% win rate against tuned-and-ensembled LightGBM/XGBoost and an 82% win rate against 4-hour-tuned AutoGluon.
  • TALENT (274 diverse datasets): TabPFN-3 ranks first in aggregate and within every individual task type (regression, binary and multiclass classification) — evidence the TabArena wins aren't an artifact of a small curated dataset selection.
  • TabSTAR (50 real text-tabular datasets): TabPFN-3-Plus (Thinking) dominates dedicated text-aware baselines (CatBoost, TabSTAR, SAP-RPT-OSS); among methods without native text support, plain TabPFN-3 is still the strongest.

Beyond core tabular prediction, the report also claims a new state-of-the-art foundation model for relational data (RelBenchV1) and up to 120× faster SHAP-value computation via the reduced KV-cache.

Continuing, not reversing, the commercial trajectory

Ships under a "TABPFN-3.0 License v1.0" — the same non-commercial-by-default pattern TabPFN-2.5 introduced (free for research/internal evaluation, paid enterprise license for production), with TabPFN-3-Plus and Thinking mode available only via API or enterprise on-prem/VPC deployment (AWS SageMaker, Azure AI Foundry). The open-source release (plain TabPFN-3) is the strongest tier available without a commercial agreement.

Discussion, Section by Section

Plain-English walkthrough (start here)

Read tabpfn.html and tabpfn25.html first. This one is different in kind from the 2→2.5 jump — that was "same design, bigger and deeper." This is "we hit a wall with the old design, so we changed the design."

1. Why the old architecture couldn't just keep scaling

Picture the earlier design: every cell in your spreadsheet gets its own little worker, and those workers take turns looking across their row, then across their column, back and forth, many times. That's expressive, but the amount of work grows with rows times columns, repeatedly — fine at 10,000 rows, painful at 1,000,000. TabPFN-3's fix: first squash each row down into a single, fixed-size summary (in two steps — first understand each column on its own, then blend a row's columns together into one vector), and only after that's done, let rows talk to each other for the actual prediction step. The expensive "everyone talks to everyone" stage now only has to deal with rows, not rows-times-columns — which is why it can suddenly handle 10× more data than before.

2. Fixing the "how many categories can you predict" ceiling

Earlier versions had a built-in classification head sized for a small, fixed number of possible answers — reasonable for "yes/no" or "which of these 8 fruits," awkward for "which of these 200 disease subtypes." TabPFN-3's new decoder instead compares a new example against every training example's known answer and does a weighted vote — essentially a smart, learned version of "find the most similar things I've already seen and go with what they were," which naturally handles however many categories show up, rather than being hard-wired to a fixed count.

3. The unglamorous but genuinely important fix: caching

TabPFN has always mashed "learn from this data" and "make a prediction" into a single step, done fresh every time. That's elegant for research, but a nightmare for a real product serving predictions all day — imagine a calculator that had to relearn arithmetic from scratch every single time you pressed "=". TabPFN-3 adds a genuine cache: do the expensive "learn from this data" step once, remember it cheaply, and reuse it for every future prediction — turning what used to be a fresh computation every time into something that runs one to a thousand times faster after the first call. This is boring-sounding but it's the difference between a research toy and something you can actually run behind an API.

4. "Thinking mode" — let it work longer on hard cases

This borrows an idea that showed up recently in language models: instead of always giving an instant answer, let the model spend extra computation thinking through a hard case before answering, and it gets measurably better at exactly the cases that benefit from it. The paper is careful to say this extra "thinking" doesn't involve a language model, real data, or internet search smuggled in on the side — it's purely more of TabPFN-3's own computation, spent more generously per prediction.

5. The prior admits its own past mistake, out loud

Worth noticing: this report states plainly that TabPFN-2.5 "struggled with high-frequency oscillations despite performing well on sinusoidal data generally" — a specific, named weakness of the previous release, fixed here. That kind of candor (naming exactly what broke, not just claiming broad improvement) is a good sign of a report worth trusting on its other specific claims too.

Going deeper (revisit once the basics above feel solid)

The new temporal/dynamic-SCM prior is worth remembering once we reach tabpfnts.html next in this reading order — TabPFN-3 explicitly ships a specialized "TabPFN-TS-3" checkpoint built on this prior, ranking 2nd on a dedicated time-series benchmark. And the explicit out-of-distribution/extrapolation prior — training the model to handle genuine distribution shift, not just interpolate within its training range — is worth tracking as a running theme against the calibration/trust concerns raised all the way back in Cranmer's and Hermans' papers: extrapolation is exactly the regime where a model's stated confidence is hardest to trust, whether that model is a tree, a PFN, or anything else.

Also worth flagging: this report's own numbers are, once again, a mix of the independently-curated TabArena/TALENT/TabSTAR public benchmarks and results specific to Prior Labs' internal, non-public evaluation suite (Section 3.2) — the same asymmetry noted for TabPFN-2.5. The public-benchmark numbers carry more evidentiary weight than the internal ones.
