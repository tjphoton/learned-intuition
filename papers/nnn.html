NNN: Neural Networks for Marketing Measurement

Mulc, Anderson, Cubre, Zhang, Liu, Kumar — Google·2025the foil
Open PDF (private repo) Back to study plan
Summary

The one existing neural alternative to classic MMM — and it goes the opposite direction from amortized inference: a Transformer trained on a brand's own data that abandons explicit priors and parametric adstock/saturation, trading uncertainty quantification for flexibility. The counterpoint for explaining why amortized SBI (priors kept, just moved into a simulator) is a different bet.

Study Notes

The core departure from Jin et al., in the paper's own four bullets

  • Rich data representation — media and organic signals become high-dimensional embeddings (Google Search queries embedded via a pretrained multilingual sentence encoder; YouTube ad creatives embedded via a pretrained video model), not scalar spend/impressions. Embedding magnitude carries volume; direction carries semantic/qualitative content — capturing ad quality, not just quantity, which is structurally invisible to a scalar-input MMM.
  • Long-term effects via an intermediary signal, not a parametric decay function — marketing's influence on Google Search (a proxy for consumer demand) is modeled explicitly and autoregressively, with a Transformer attending across the full history, instead of a hand-specified adstock curve that (per the paper) "typically lose[s] identifiability... beyond a few months."
  • L1 regularization is what makes training a model this heavily parameterized feasible in the same data-constrained setting that has historically blocked deep learning approaches to MMM.
  • Objective, OOD-driven model selection — MAPE/R² on a temporally distinct held-out test set, rather than the parameter-plausibility judgment calls MMM model selection typically leans on.

Architecture: one tensor, factored attention, no positional encoding, no layer norm

All inputs — scalars and embeddings alike — unify into one rank-4 tensor (Geo, Time, Channel, EmbeddingDim), scalars simply zero-padded to the embedding width. A custom "Factored Self-Attention" splits into two independent, multiplied components: Temporal Attention (a learned function of the time-gap between query and key, replacing a hand-specified adstock curve with a learned smooth decay — the paper reports it recovers smooth, adstock-like decay shapes without being told to) and Channel Attention (a fixed, learned C×C interaction matrix, initialized to the identity — starting from "a channel mostly explains its own past," then learning cross-channel influence during training). Two deliberate omissions, both explicitly justified: no positional encoding (avoids anchoring to an arbitrary start date and avoids poor extrapolation beyond the training time range) and no layer normalization (removed specifically because the embedding vectors' magnitude encodes real marketing-volume information that normalization would destroy).

Two prediction heads — and why the Search head matters more than it first appears

A Sales head (built on a prior CoSMo function: Search-embedding direction → sigmoid MLP → conversion-rate-like probability, scaled by Search volume, extended additively across channels) and a Search head that predicts the next period's search embedding (magnitude and direction) autoregressively. The Search head is what lets NNN separate a channel's direct effect on sales from its indirect effect (channel → search → sales) — a decomposition a standard MMM structurally cannot make, since it typically treats Search as an exogenous control variable rather than a causal mediator.

Results: dramatic gains exactly where the embeddings should matter, and an honest real-world warning

  • Simulated Low-Variance data: NNN test R²=0.95 vs. MMM's 0.44 — NNN explains far more held-out variance despite similar MAPE.
  • Simulated High-Variance data (deliberately includes creative-effectiveness variation MMM's scalar inputs cannot see at all): NNN test MAPE 3.2% vs. MMM's 15% — attributed directly to NNN's embeddings correctly distinguishing high-impact from low-impact search terms, something a volume-only representation cannot do even in principle.
  • Attribution accuracy vs. known ground truth (possible because the data is simulated): NNN's autoregressive-unrolling variant correctly shifts attribution from Search to YouTube, crediting YouTube's indirect effect through Search — though honestly imperfect, landing at 39.65%–55.10% of the true 46.29% total YouTube contribution across the two variance regimes, not exact.
  • A genuinely important cross-cutting finding: as L1 regularization relaxes, predictive error and attribution error improve together up to a point, then degrade together past the onset of overfitting. This is the empirical justification for NNN's whole model-selection philosophy — minimizing out-of-distribution predictive error turns out to be a good proxy for accurate causal attribution too, at least in these experiments.
  • Real-world regional data — the sharpest, most concrete result in the paper: the baseline MMM does reasonably in-sample (20% validation MAPE) but catastrophically fails to generalize to a genuinely out-of-time-period test set: 189% test MAPE, R² = −0.90 — worse than just predicting the mean. NNN's baseline achieves 22.5% test MAPE / 0.95 R²; the full model (two more embedded channels added) reaches 10.0% test MAPE / 0.98 R². A concrete, striking demonstration of exactly the temporal-generalization fragility Jin et al.'s own paper worried about in principle.
  • An honest negative finding: probing the trained embeddings (via t-SNE) to check whether the model learned to distinguish high- from low-impact search queries worked clearly on the High-Variance dataset but showed almost no discrimination on the Low-Variance dataset — the paper's own explanation: "because the low variance dataset lacks sufficient keyword variation, the model cannot effectively learn the correlation between query content and sales." A model can't learn a signal that isn't meaningfully present in its training data — reported directly rather than glossed over.

Where the priors actually went — the paper's own precise framing (Section 13)

Not "no priors" — priors relocated. Bayesian MMM encodes belief as explicit, parameter-level probability distributions. NNN encodes belief three other ways instead: architectural choices (the causal attention mask hard-codes "the future cannot influence the past"; removing layer normalization hard-codes "embedding magnitude carries real information"; identity-matrix initialization of the channel-attention matrix soft-codes "a channel mostly explains its own past, initially"); and the L1 penalty, which embeds an implicit sparsity belief — "many parameters are ultimately unnecessary" — chosen primarily for its regularization benefit rather than to encode specific, quantifiable beliefs about individual parameter values. The paper is explicit that these architectural priors are not equivalent in kind to Bayesian MMM's granular, inspectable, per-parameter priors.

An honestly named, significant gap: no uncertainty quantification at all, currently

Stated directly: "Bayesian MMMs offer a significant advantage in inherently quantifying uncertainty... NNN provides neither uncertainty estimates of the model parameters nor the predictions" in its current form — trained via point-estimate gradient descent, not posterior sampling. Monte Carlo Dropout is named as a possible future direction, not something implemented. Measured against literally the entire calibration/trust theme running through Module 03 of this library, NNN as described here has none of that machinery yet — a real, acknowledged, currently-open gap, not a minor footnote.

The paper's own summary verdict — genuinely not triumphalist

"We see NNN as a complement rather than a replacement to MMM, with additional capabilities for channels with embedding data but a greater need for research and understanding of its uncertainties." Explicit, named limitations for future work: no cost data / ROAS calculation yet (volume-based attribution only, missing Jin et al.'s entire ROAS/mROAS/optimal-mix apparatus); tested with only two embedding-based channels so far (Search Ads, YouTube) against MMM's traditional ability to model dozens of scalar channels at once; needs live-experiment validation, not just retrospective/simulated results; and embedding-aggregation methods (simple summation currently) could likely be improved with attention-based pooling.

Discussion, Section by Section

Plain-English walkthrough (start here)

Read jinmmm.html first — this paper is written explicitly as a response to it, sometimes citing it directly, and makes the most sense read as a direct counterpoint rather than standalone.

1. Teaching a model to tell a good ad from a bland one

The older approach only ever tells the model "you spent $10,000 on this ad" — a single number, with zero information about whether the ad was any good. This paper's central bet: represent each ad, and each batch of search activity, not as a number but as a rich fingerprint capturing both how much happened and what it actually was (which words people searched, what the ad creative actually showed). The payoff shows up exactly where you'd predict — on the dataset deliberately built to include ad-quality variation, this richer representation wins by a wide margin; on the dataset without that variation, both approaches do about equally well, because there's nothing extra to learn in that case.

2. Watching what an ad does to curiosity, not just to sales

A TV ad might not make anyone buy something the same day — but it might make people search for the product a week later, and that search behavior is what eventually turns into a sale. The older approach can't see this two-step chain at all; it only sees "ad spend" and "sales" as two separate columns. This paper adds a second target the model has to predict — what happens to search activity — turning it into a mediating step the model explicitly reasons through, rather than folding everything into one opaque coefficient.

3. Beliefs baked into the blueprint instead of written on a form

The older approach makes you fill out an explicit form before fitting the model: "I believe this parameter is probably around this value, with this much uncertainty." This paper's approach skips that form, but it isn't belief-free — the beliefs just get built into the model's construction instead of written down as numbers. Preventing the model from looking into the future is a belief, hard-wired into the architecture. Starting the model off assuming each channel mostly predicts itself, before training nudges it otherwise, is a belief too. It's a real, meaningful difference in how beliefs get encoded, not a case of one approach having beliefs and the other having none.

4. The most honest sentence in the whole paper

Worth reading directly rather than paraphrasing away: the authors say plainly that their new model currently provides no sense of how confident it should be — no error bars on its parameters, none on its predictions. Given how much of this entire library is about exactly that gap (does a model know what it doesn't know), that's a genuinely significant, clearly-flagged limitation, not a minor asterisk. A model that predicts brilliantly but can't tell you how much to trust any single prediction is a different kind of tool than one that can.

5. A real-world result worth sitting with

The single most dramatic number in this paper isn't from a simulation — it's from real regional sales data, where the traditional approach did fine on data similar to what it was trained on, then fell apart completely on data from a later time period, actually performing worse than just guessing the average every time. That's the practical stakes made concrete: a model can look trustworthy right up until the moment reality shifts slightly, and the only way to know in advance is to specifically test on data unlike anything the model has seen — exactly the discipline this paper argues for as its primary model-selection criterion.

Going deeper (revisit once the basics above feel solid)

Worth holding the full picture from Module 05 together once discretechoice.html (next) is also covered: Jin et al. is the careful, uncertainty-aware incumbent that admits its own small-sample weaknesses; this paper is the flexible, embedding-rich alternative that admits its own uncertainty-quantification weakness; and neither one is the amortized, portfolio-pooling answer this whole library's own MMM thread is actually building toward (see metabeta.html and Module 05's own framing of the still-open gap). Worth explicitly asking, once all three papers are fresh: could NNN's rich embeddings and metabeta's amortized hierarchical pooling be combined — an amortized network trained across many brands' worth of simulated data, that also takes creative-quality embeddings as input? Nobody in this reading order has built that yet.
